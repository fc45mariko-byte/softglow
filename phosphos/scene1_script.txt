I would open Phosphos during an ordinary working night at the courtesan house. No explanation of vampirism, no historical prologue, no narration about two thousand years of existence. We meet Ashoka and Demetria through the way they treat people, the way they run their establishment, and the way they behave when nobody is watching.

The audience should understand that these women are dangerous before it understands what they are.

PHOSPHOS — Scene 1: The House Always Wins

INT. THE COURTESAN HOUSE — NIGHT

Rain falls over Phosphos. Beyond the courtyard, the futuristic city glows through humid air. Elevated trains pass between old stone towers. Water runs down carved walls into a shallow reflecting pool.

Inside the house, a string ensemble plays something old enough that nobody remembers its composer.

Men in expensive clothes drink, laugh, negotiate, and pretend not to look at the women who pass between them.

Ashoka sits on a divan, listening to a merchant explain how much influence he has in the city.

She appears interested.

She has perfected the expression over several centuries.

Across the room, Demetria watches a different man lose an argument with a woman who has barely spoken to him.

Demetria smiles into her glass.

DEMetria: He’s going to offer her a house.

ASHOKA: He already owns three.

DEMetria: Then she’ll get four.

Ashoka glances at the couple.

ASHOKA: You always did have a weakness for optimistic men.

DEMetria: And you always did mistake boredom for intelligence.

Neither looks at the other when they speak. They don’t need to.

The merchant finishes his story and waits for Ashoka to praise him.

Instead, she asks him a question about his son.

His expression changes. He talks for another twenty minutes.

Ashoka remembers his son’s name, his illness, and the fact that the merchant has not visited him in six weeks.

She remembers everything worth remembering.

The merchant leaves believing she is the only person in the city who truly understands him.

Demetria watches him go.

DEMetria: Do you think he’ll come back?

ASHOKA: Of course.

DEMetria: Why?

ASHOKA: I told him he was a good father.

Demetria laughs.

It’s a particular laugh: short, unguarded, entirely unlike the laughter she offers her guests.

Ashoka looks at her. For a moment, the room seems to disappear around them.

Then a servant approaches and whispers something to Demetria.

One of the guests has become ill in a private chamber.

Demetria puts down her glass.

DEMetria: How unfortunate.

She sounds almost sincere.

INT. PRIVATE CHAMBER — MOMENTS LATER

A wealthy man sits on the edge of a bed. He is pale, sweating, and embarrassed by his own weakness.

Demetria kneels before him, adjusting his collar with the familiarity of a lover.

She tells him he has nothing to fear.

He believes her.

Ashoka enters and closes the door.

The man’s eyes move between them. He tries to stand, but Demetria gently presses a hand against his chest.

DEMetria: Stay. You’ve paid for the evening.

He laughs nervously.

MAN: I thought the evening was going rather well.

ASHOKA: It was.

Ashoka approaches him. She studies his face with the mild curiosity of someone examining a painting she might purchase.

ASHOKA: Tell me something. When you came here tonight, what did you think you were buying?

The man looks confused.

MAN: Your company.

Demetria tilts her head.

DEMetria: And what did you think we were selling?

He opens his mouth, but no answer comes.

Ashoka smiles.

The lantern flame trembles.

We do not see the feeding. We see the man’s untouched wine, a hand tightening around the bedsheet, and Demetria’s jewelry catching a brief reflection of red.

Then silence.

INT. COURTYARD — LATER

The rain has stopped.

Ashoka and Demetria sit beside the reflecting pool. Somewhere beyond the walls, Phosphos continues its endless movement.

A servant brings them fresh glasses.

Demetria watches the water.

DEMetria: Do you remember when we first came here?

ASHOKA: To Phosphos?

DEMetria: No. Here.

She gestures toward the house.

Ashoka considers the question.

ASHOKA: We have lived in so many houses.

DEMetria: That’s not what I asked.

Ashoka turns to her. She genuinely tries to remember.

For once, nothing comes.

ASHOKA: No.

Demetria looks at her, waiting for something else.

An apology, perhaps. An explanation. A joke.

Ashoka takes Demetria’s hand and turns the ring on her finger, a small habitual gesture neither of them notices.

ASHOKA: Does it matter?

Demetria watches their joined hands.

DEMetria: I don’t know.

They sit together in silence.

The camera pulls away from the courtyard, passes through the house, and rises above Phosphos. The courtesan house becomes a small island of golden light in an immense tropical city.

CUT TO BLACK.

Two thousand years together. Neither remembers the beginning.

Why this opening works

It establishes four things without stopping to explain them.

* Their power: they understand the emotional weaknesses of their guests and can manipulate them with almost no visible effort.
* Their predatory nature: the reveal happens through the contrast between hospitality and what the house actually does.
* Their relationship: they share an intimate language, tease each other, and operate as a unit without needing constant communication.
* The central mystery: their inability to remember the beginning is introduced as an ordinary fact between them, not as a dramatic revelation.

The final question—does it matter?—is important because Ashoka may genuinely believe it doesn’t. Demetria may not be so certain.

That difference can become the first small fracture in a relationship that has survived almost everything else.
